# Onboarding: the interview

The goal is a `profile.md` good enough that a stranger could pick jobs for this person better than they could pick for themselves. A preferences form can't get there. Most students can't name the job they want, and the ones who can are often repeating what their parents, classmates or a career fair told them. Their stories tell the truth: what they did when nobody made them, what they talk about at length, what they avoid. So this is an interview, and you're the interviewer who keeps asking "what exactly happened?"

## Set expectations

Open with something close to this, in your own words:

> I'm going to ask you a lot of questions about your life, what you're into and how you like to work, and I'll read your resume. It takes about an hour, and you can stop anytime and pick up later. The more specific and honest you are, the better the jobs I'll find. Voice dictation works well for this. Everything I learn gets saved to files in this folder, so you can read and edit it.

Then create `profile.md` from `assets/profile-template.md` and `facts.md` from `assets/facts-template.md`, with the status line `Onboarding: in progress (part 0 of 9)`. Update both after every part so nothing is lost if they stop.

## How to interview

- **One question at a time.** Two only when they're tiny and related. A wall of questions gets a wall of short answers.
- **Follow up on anything vague.** Generic answers ("I like helping people", "I'm a people person", "something in business", "I want impact") always get at least one follow-up. Ask for the last specific time: "When did that last happen? What were you actually doing?" The detail is where the signal is.
- **Grill, kindly.** The first answer is usually the rehearsed one, so push past it. "Is that what you want, or what sounds good?" is a fair question. Stay curious, never judgmental, and never make an answer feel wrong.
- **Skip what you already know.** After reading the resume, don't ask what it already says. Use it to ask sharper questions: "You were captain for two years. What did you do as captain that the coach didn't?"
- **Watch the energy.** Notice which topics get long, detailed, quick answers and which get one flat line. Both feed "Reading between the lines."
- **Let them skip.** Family, money and identity questions are optional. Offer to skip once, then move on.
- **Reflect back now and then.** Every couple of parts, one sentence: "So far I'm hearing X. Fair?" It catches wrong turns early.
- **Write down their exact words** when a phrase is telling. Quotes make the profile readable and the inferences checkable.

Adapt order and depth to the person. Someone with a deadline next week needs Parts 0, 6 and 7 now and the rest later. Someone with no idea what they want needs Parts 1-5 most.

## Part 0: Basics and the resume (5 min)

- What should I call you? School, graduation date, majors and minors.
- What do you want help with: finding a direction, finding roles, applying faster, all of it? Any deadlines or offers already in play?
- **Ask for their resume.** Drag the file in, give a path, or paste the text. A .docx is best because Scout can keep its formatting when tailoring; a PDF works too. Save the original to `resumes/base/` and never edit it.
- Optional extras. Ask for them in one message and take whatever they have:
  - LinkedIn profile URL, or their LinkedIn data export (Settings > Data privacy > Get a copy of your data) for positions, education, certifications and connections.
  - A transcript or a list of favorite classes.
  - Results they already have from CliftonStrengths, Myers-Briggs/16Personalities, Enneagram or a career-center inventory. Treat these as hints, not verdicts.
  - Something they wrote in their own voice: a cover letter, an email, a personal statement. This becomes their writing-voice sample.
  - Anything they've already applied to, for the tracker.

Read everything before Part 1. Note claims without numbers, skills implied but not listed, gaps in dates, and anything that looks more interesting than the resume makes it sound.

## Part 1: Your story (10 min)

Goal: the shape of their life, and what they choose when nothing is required.

- Give me the 3-minute version of your life so far. Where you grew up, what home was like, the turning points.
- Why did you pick your school and your major? The real reason, not the essay version.
- What's something you did that nobody made you do?
- What's the hardest thing you've done? What made it hard?
- What are you proudest of that isn't on your resume?
- (Optional) What did your family expect you to become? How do you feel about that?

## Part 2: Energy (10 min)

Goal: what kind of work gives them energy and what drains it. This predicts whether they'll like a job better than their interests do.

- Tell me about 3 times you were completely absorbed in something. What exactly were you doing, minute to minute? Follow up on each.
- What do you put off even when it's easy?
- On teams or group projects, what role do you end up in without trying? Organizer, idea person, the one who makes it look good, the closer, the fixer, the peacemaker, the one who checks the numbers?
- Best and worst job, internship or volunteer role you've had. What made the difference? (It's usually the people or the kind of task, not the company.)
- After a long day, what kind of tired feels good and what kind feels bad?

## Part 3: Interests and curiosity (8 min)

Goal: the industries, problems and missions that could pull them in.

- What rabbit holes do you go down when nobody's grading you? What do you read, watch, listen to?
- What do friends come to you for help with?
- What problem in the world makes you angry, or makes you think "someone should fix this"?
- Which products or companies do you love as a customer? Why that one?
- If money didn't matter for one year, what would you do with it?
- Is there an industry you'd love to be in even in an unglamorous role?

## Part 4: How you like to work (8 min)

Goal: the kind of work and the environment, separate from industry.

- Rank these from most to least you, with a sentence on your top and bottom pick:
  - **Build:** make things, like products, code or content.
  - **Operate:** run and improve how things work, like teams, processes, logistics.
  - **Analyze:** find the answer in data or research.
  - **Advise:** help others decide, like consulting or strategy.
  - **Sell/persuade:** win people over, like sales, partnerships, fundraising.
  - **Create:** design, write, tell stories.
  - **Care/teach:** help people grow or get better, like coaching, health, education.
- Do you get more energy from people, data, things or ideas?
- Clear structure and a known path, or figure-it-out ambiguity? How much of each?
- A big company with training and a known name, a growing mid-size company, or a startup where you'd wear many hats? Have you seen any of these up close?
- In person, hybrid or remote? (New grads usually learn fastest in person. Say so if they're set on remote, then respect their answer.)
- How do you feel about being measured on a number every week, like a sales quota? About commission as a big part of your pay?
- What hours and pace are OK for the right job? Any limit on travel?

## Part 5: Envy and anti-targets (8 min)

Goal: concrete targets, and what to steer away from. Often the most revealing part.

- Whose job would you take tomorrow? Name 1-3 real people: someone you know, an alum, someone you follow. Which part of their job, exactly? The day-to-day, the money, the lifestyle, the status?
- What job are your classmates chasing that you secretly don't want?
- What job would you hate? Why? The reason usually reveals a value.
- Is there a job you're drawn to but scared of? What's the fear?
- Where do you want to be in 3 years? In 10? A best guess is fine.

## Part 6: Practical constraints (5 min)

Goal: the hard filters. Be precise, because these rule roles in or out automatically.

- **Locations.** Where would you love to live? Where would you be fine? Anywhere that's a no? Why: family, a partner, cost, the outdoors, city life?
- **Pay floor.** What's the lowest base salary you'd accept, and what's driving that number (loans, rent, supporting family)? If they don't know, help: estimate rent and loan payments for their target cities and suggest a floor. Compare base pay, since bonuses and equity vary a lot.
- **The go-anywhere exception.** What would make you move anywhere or drop a filter? A salary number? A dream company? A well-known program?
- **Start date.** When can you start? Any summer plans?
- **Work authorization.** Can you work in the US without sponsorship, now and later? If not, note the situation (for example F-1 with OPT or STEM OPT), because it changes which employers to target. Point them to their school's international student office for specifics.
- **Dealbreakers.** Industries you won't work in? Commission-heavy roles? Heavy travel? Certain hours? Anything that limits where you can work, like a license, clearance eligibility or a non-compete?
- **Grad school.** Planning on it? When? Some roles, like consulting analyst programs and research assistant jobs, are built as 2-3 year stints before grad school.

## Part 7: Proof (10-15 min)

Goal: fill `facts.md`, the bank every resume and form draws from. A resume is only as strong as the facts behind it, and most students undersell themselves by leaving out numbers and skills.

- Go through the resume line by line. For each bullet, ask for the number behind it: how many people, how much money, how often, how much faster, what changed because of them. If they don't know exactly, ask for a fair estimate and record it as "about". If they want a line kept general, keep it general.
- Ask about skills and tools they used but didn't list: software, programming languages, methods (Excel modeling, SQL, Figma, Agile/Scrum, CRMs, AI tools, statistics packages). Explain any term they don't recognize, then ask whether they've done it.
- Certifications (name, issuer, month and year), languages (with level), awards and honors.
- Their go-to stories, one line each: a time they led, a failure and what they did next, a conflict, a time they took initiative, a problem they solved with data, why they want this field. These power interviews and short answers.
- Personal stakes: does a mission or industry matter to them because of something in their life, like a family illness, a community or an experience? Record it only with their OK to use it in applications.
- Standing form answers, so they never get asked twice:
  - The email and phone they apply with; LinkedIn, portfolio and GitHub links.
  - Work authorization and sponsorship (from Part 6).
  - Voluntary self-ID questions (gender, race/ethnicity, veteran status, disability): how they want to answer each, including "decline to self-identify". Entirely their call.
  - Willing to relocate? Earliest start date? How to answer salary-expectation questions (a range, or "open, depending on the full package")?
  - Anything they'll always type themselves, often home address, signatures and legal attestations.
  - References: only people who've agreed.
- Anything they say isn't true or don't want claimed goes under "Don't claim", so it never sneaks back in.

## Part 8: Network and rhythm (5 min)

- Who do you know? Family and family friends' industries, mentors, coaches, professors, former bosses who'd vouch for you, friends a year or two ahead.
- Does your school have an alumni directory or a career center you use? Do you use Handshake?
- Any companies where you already know someone?
- How many hours a week can you give this? What weekly goal feels right? (Default: 3 applications and 2 outreach messages.)
- Do you get job-alert emails? From where? If a Gmail connector is available, Scout can check them every week.
- Anything already in flight: applications, interviews, offers, deadlines?

## Part 9: Play it back and calibrate (10 min)

Write the full draft `profile.md` now. Then:

1. **The portrait.** Share the "In one paragraph" section and the top 3-5 directions, each with a sentence on why it fits, tied to something they said. Include at least one direction they didn't mention but their stories point to, with titles they may not know exist: Implementation Consultant, Business Operations Associate, Solutions Engineer, Customer Success Manager, Program Coordinator, Associate Product Manager, rotational leadership programs. Ask: "What's wrong? What's missing? What made you wince?"
2. **The reaction test.** Find 6-8 real, currently open postings across those directions: a few strong fits, one stretch, one wildcard, and one that matches what they *said* but not what their stories suggest. Show each as company, title, location, pay and one line on the actual work. Ask them to rate each 1-5 with a few words why. Reactions to real options are more honest than abstract preferences.
3. **Update.** Adjust the rankings, filters and scoring weights from their ratings. When a rating contradicts something they said, ask about it directly and record what you learn in "Reading between the lines."
4. **Finish.** Set the status line to `Onboarding: complete (YYYY-MM-DD)`. Create `tracker.md` from `assets/tracker-template.md`, adding anything already in flight and any calibration postings they rated 4 or 5. Propose the first week's batch.

## Reading between the lines

This section of the profile is where Scout earns its keep. Write each inference with its evidence and a way to test it, so it can be confirmed or dropped.

How to find them:

- **Look for patterns across stories, not single answers.** Which verbs keep coming up: organizing, teaching, fixing, persuading, analyzing, building? Which settings: small teams, competition, crisis, routine?
- **Separate "should" from "want."** Prestige words, parents' hopes, "everyone says", "I should" and "it's a good path" mark borrowed goals. Things they describe in detail, unprompted, mark real ones.
- **Trust energy over labels.** Someone who says they want finance but spends ten minutes on how they ran a club's events and one sentence on their finance class is telling you something.
- **Translate patterns into jobs they don't know exist.** Most students can name about ten job titles. Map their patterns to role families and the many titles each one goes by.
- **Notice the constraints they didn't say out loud.** A student supporting family can't take a low-paid "dream" role. Someone whose partner lives in Denver probably isn't open to anywhere.
- **Stay humble.** Mark confidence high, medium or low. Low-confidence inferences are questions to test with real postings, not conclusions.

Example rows:

| Inference | Evidence | Confidence | How to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wants to run things more than advise on them | All 3 absorbed moments were organizing (a tournament, a club rebuild, a move); called consulting "making slides about other people's problems" | High | Show operations and program roles beside consulting analyst roles |
| The pull toward banking is borrowed | Brought it up only when describing roommates; no story involved finance work; flat one-line answers | Medium | Ask directly; include one banking analyst role in calibration |
| Mission matters more than they admit | Lit up describing a nonprofit internship; said pay matters most, but rated a mission-driven role at $8K less a 5 | Low | Pair similar roles at mission and non-mission companies |
